# Commissioning paid work: IP terms

**Read this before hiring anyone**, including on Fiverr, Upwork or any
freelance platform.

> Not legal advice. These clauses are a starting point drafted by a
> non-lawyer; have a solicitor review them before you rely on them for
> anything you intend to sell. Employment and IP law differ by country, and
> the contractor's country matters as much as yours.

---

## Why this matters here specifically

**By default, in most jurisdictions, the person who writes the code owns the
copyright in it — even when you paid them for it.** "Work made for hire" is a
narrow doctrine in the United States and does not exist in that form in the UK
or most of the EU. Paying an invoice buys you the deliverable; it does not
automatically buy you the copyright unless the contract says so.

For an ordinary app that is a nuisance. For this project it is worse, because
of the dual licence:

- The AGPL side is fine — a contractor who writes AGPL code and delivers it has
  given you something you can ship under the AGPL.
- **The commercial side breaks.** If the contractor owns the copyright in the
  Android layer, you cannot sell anyone a commercial licence covering it. You
  would be selling rights you do not hold. Discovering that after you have
  taken money is considerably worse than discovering it now.

Freelance platforms' default terms are thin on this and vary by platform and by
plan. Do not rely on them. Put it in the contract.

## Clauses to include

Paste these into the agreement, adjusting names and law:

### 1. Assignment

> The Contractor hereby assigns to the Client, absolutely and with full title
> guarantee, all right, title and interest in and to all copyright, database
> rights, design rights and all other intellectual property rights, whether
> registered or unregistered and anywhere in the world, in all work product
> created by the Contractor in the course of performing this agreement (the
> "Work Product"), including all source code, build configuration, designs,
> documentation and test material. This assignment takes effect on creation of
> each part of the Work Product, and applies whether or not the Client has paid
> for it at that time.

### 2. Moral rights

> To the fullest extent permitted by law, the Contractor waives all moral
> rights in the Work Product. Where such rights cannot be waived, the
> Contractor agrees not to assert them against the Client or its licensees.

### 3. Further assurance

> The Contractor shall, at the Client's request and expense, execute any
> documents and do any acts necessary to vest the rights assigned above in the
> Client, or to register or perfect them, both during and after this agreement.

### 4. Pre-existing and third-party material

> The Contractor shall not incorporate into the Work Product any material owned
> by a third party, or any of the Contractor's own pre-existing material,
> without the Client's prior written consent. Where such consent is given, the
> Contractor grants (or shall procure) a perpetual, worldwide, irrevocable,
> royalty-free, sublicensable licence to use that material as part of the Work
> Product, and shall identify it and its licence in writing on delivery.

This one is not boilerplate. **Ask for a written list of every third-party
dependency added, with its licence, as a delivery condition.** A single
copyleft library pulled in for convenience — an AGPL Tor wrapper, a GPL I2P
binding — makes the commercial licence unsellable, and it will not be obvious
from the diff.

### 5. Confidentiality and no residual rights

> The Contractor shall keep confidential all non-public information disclosed
> by the Client and shall retain no copy of the Work Product after delivery
> other than as required by law. Nothing in this agreement grants the
> Contractor any right to use the Work Product, or the Client's existing
> codebase, in any other project.

### 6. Security-specific warranties

> The Contractor warrants that it will not weaken, bypass or reimplement any
> cryptographic or security-relevant function of the Client's existing Rust
> core, nor substitute an alternative cryptographic implementation, without the
> Client's prior written approval.

Worth stating explicitly. "It was easier to bind the platform crypto library"
is a plausible thing for a competent Android developer to do, and it would
undo the property the project is built around.

## Deliverables to demand

1. Every commit signed off (`git commit -s`), per [CLA.md](CLA.md) — the
   assignment above governs, but the sign-off gives you a per-commit record.
2. A written third-party dependency list with licences, per clause 4.
3. A written statement of which parts, if any, were substantially generated by
   an AI tool.

## The awkward one: does anyone own this code?

Your README states plainly that the codebase is AI-generated under your
direction. That is honest and it should stay. It also raises a question you
should get advice on **before** you sell a commercial licence rather than
after.

Several jurisdictions require human authorship for copyright to exist. The US
Copyright Office has refused registration for works it considered generated
without sufficient human authorship, and has said that a prompt alone may not
be enough. The UK has a specific provision for computer-generated works
(CDPA s.9(3)), which many other countries do not. The position differs by
country and is actively being litigated.

What this means in practice:

- **The AGPL side is unaffected in any way that matters.** If the code turns
  out not to be copyrightable, the practical result is that it is freely
  usable — which is close to what the AGPL achieves anyway.
- **The commercial side depends on it.** Selling an exclusive right presumes
  there is a right to sell. If material portions are held not to be
  copyrightable, a commercial licence conveys less than a buyer expects.

This is not a reason to abandon the plan. Direction, selection, arrangement,
protocol design, the specification, the test suite and the debugging judgement
are all human contributions, and the argument that the whole is a work of human
authorship is a real one. But it is an argument, and it is worth having a
lawyer make it properly — and worth being careful, in the commercial licence
terms, not to warrant more than you can stand behind.

**Concretely: get advice before you take money, not before you post the job.**
Hiring a contractor under a proper assignment is sound regardless of how that
question resolves. It only makes your position stronger.
